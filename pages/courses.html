Interferometric Computational Imaging

This course will provide an introduction to interferometry, a classical imaging methodology with applications ranging from retinal scanning in ophthalmology, to coherent lidar in autonomous vehicles, and to gravitational wave detection in astronomy. More recently, interferometric computational imaging methods have enabled capabilities such as passive lidar and quadrillion FPS videos. We will learn about the physics of interferometry and coherent imaging, and emerging computational imaging applications. We will also get hands-on experience working with interferometric systems, by building, calibrating, and capturing data with a low-cost interferometer capable of 3D scanning at micrometer resolutions.

Offered: 2025 · Toronto 2026 · Princeton

Single-Photon Computational Imaging

Single-photon avalanche diodes (SPADs) are an emerging class of image sensors capable of detecting individual photons, at timing resolutions from picoseconds to nanoseconds. SPAD sensors are commonly used in active imaging applications such as lidar, and are becoming increasingly popular in compelling passive imaging applications. In this course, we provide an introduction to SPADs, how they capture light, their imaging applications, and the hardware trends that are influencing the future outlook of these sensors and research directions in this space. The course includes a strong hands-on component featuring a low-cost ($20) SPAD sensor used for active imaging, and a quarter-megapixel SPAD sensor used for passive imaging.

Adaptive Optics: Principles and Applications

Adaptive optics (AO) enables optical systems to measure and correct aberrations in real time, improving image quality in astronomy, microscopy, and other imaging applications. In this course, students will first learn the basic principles and historical development of adaptive optics, and then build and operate a compact AO system consisting of a spatial light modulator, a wavefront sensor, and a camera. We will implement closed-loop aberration correction in two ways: 1) using an existing Hartmann wavefront sensor for sensor-based control; and 2) using image-quality optimization for sensor-less control without direct wavefront sensing. Through these two approaches, students will learn the basic hardware, calibration, control, and practical trade-offs of real AO systems.

Cryo-EM Reconstruction

Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) has emerged as a leading method for visualizing biological structures at near-atomic resolution. The technique produces large collections of noisy projection images from randomly oriented molecules embedded in vitreous ice, requiring advanced computational methods to recover 3D structure. This tutorial will introduce the computational foundations of cryo-EM, including image formation, reconstruction, heterogeneity analysis, and recent machine learning approaches. We will discuss how modern algorithms address challenges such as low signal-to-noise ratios, unknown viewing directions, and molecular flexibility or heterogeneity. The tutorial is intended for researchers interested in computational imaging, inverse problems, computer vision, and machine learning applications in structural biology.

Offered: 2026 · Princeton

Instructor: Gordon Wetzstein

Build Your Own VR Headset

Students will learn about hardware (optics, electronics, display, microcontroller) and software (JavaScript, WebGL, GLSL) aspects of Virtual Reality (VR). The goal of this course is to learn all of these aspects in a hands-on manner. Each student or small team of students will build a fully functional head mounted display (HMD), including optics, display, IMU, rendering, lens distortion shader, model loader, etc., from off-the-shelf parts. The HMD we will build is inspired by the Oculus DK1. We will not learn game development, but we will study and implement the fundamental building blocks of VR systems.

Offered: 2025 · Toronto

EHT Black-Hole Imaging

The Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) can observe distant black holes through a global network of telescopes. However, EHT measurements are sparse and noisy, requiring computational algorithms to recover a black hole image. The goal of this course is to help students get comfortable with EHT data and image reconstruction algorithms. We will learn how to use the eht-imaging library and diffusion methods to (1) generate synthetic data, (2) reconstruct a synthetic image, and (3) reconstruct an image of the M87 black hole from real EHT data. These steps are a starting point towards more challenging black-hole imaging topics, including polarization, dynamic imaging, and 3D imaging.
